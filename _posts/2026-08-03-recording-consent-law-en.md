Before using a recording app, most people pause on the same question. Am I allowed to do this?

In the US the answer starts out simple and then gets complicated in one specific way. The starting point is clear enough, though.

<p class="pull">One line does most of the work: were you in the conversation?</p>

## The federal baseline

The federal Wiretap Act permits recording a communication when one party to it consents. If you are in the conversation, you are that party. Your own consent is enough.

The same statute treats intercepting a communication you are **not** part of as a crime. Penalties run up to five years, and there is civil exposure on top with a statutory floor in the thousands.

So at the federal level the split is the same one that shows up in most countries: being in the room versus not being in the room.

## Where it stops being simple

Federal law is a floor, not a ceiling. States can be stricter, and about a dozen are.

In those states, everyone in the conversation has to agree before recording starts. That is why you hear the recorded announcement at the start of customer service calls — it is one way of collecting consent from the other side.

Two things make this messier than a list suggests.

**Published lists disagree.** Depending on which source you read, states like Nevada, Michigan, and Oregon appear on one side or the other. Some of that is because those states apply different rules to phone calls than to in-person conversations. Which states are actually settled, and why the counts range from nine to fifteen, is worked through in [the state-by-state breakdown](/blog/en/state-recording-consent/).

**Calls cross state lines.** If you are in a one-party state and the person you are talking to is not, there is no clean answer. The cautious assumption is that the stricter rule governs.

The practical version: knowing the federal rule tells you the lowest standard that could ever apply. It does not tell you the standard that actually applies to you.

## Recording legally is not the end of it

Even where the recording itself is lawful, what you do with the file afterwards is a separate question.

Sharing or publishing a recording can create problems the recording did not — defamation, privacy claims, or contract and employment issues depending on the setting. "Legal to record" and "no consequences" are different statements.

## Outside the US

The gap widens fast.

Germany treats recording a private conversation without consent as a criminal matter even for participants. France is strict as well. Korea and Japan sit closer to the US federal baseline, allowing a participant to record their own conversation.

If you travel or work across borders, the rule that matters is the one where the conversation happens.

## One more layer: your employer

Even in a one-party state, a workplace policy can prohibit recording, and being within the law does not protect you from discipline. Whether such a policy is itself enforceable is a federal labour law question, covered in [a separate article](/blog/en/workplace-recording-policy/).
