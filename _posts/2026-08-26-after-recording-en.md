Starting a recording is the hard part. What comes after looks simple.

It isn't. **Most of the trouble happens after the recording, not during it.**

<p class="pull">Far more people have been hurt by what they did with a recording than by having made one.</p>

## First, the thing not to do

This comes first because it is the part that cannot be undone.

**Don't circulate it.**

Making a recording and publishing it are treated as separate acts almost everywhere. Even where the recording itself was lawful, posting it in a group chat or putting it online can amount to defamation or an invasion of privacy. The line is drawn the same way in [the US](/blog/en/recording-consent-law/), [Spain](https://hcompany-kr.github.io/blog/es/recording-consent-law/), [Italy](https://hcompany-kr.github.io/blog/it/registrare-conversazione/) and [Japan](https://hcompany-kr.github.io/blog/ja/recording-consent-law/).

**One working rule covers it: a recording is submitted to a proceeding, not shared.**

Sending it to a colleague, posting it in a family thread, attaching it to a forum post — none of those are submission. They are distribution.

## Keep the original

If only an edited file exists, the other side can argue it was manipulated. If the original exists, you can show that the edit was a straightforward excerpt.

TalkSafe's **trim function does not touch the original.** The selected section is saved as a new file and the full recording stays where it was. That means you can prepare a submission copy without losing what it came from.

The sequence worth following:

**Leave the original alone.** Don't rename it or change its date.

**Trim out only what matters.** Handing over a two-hour file is usually worse than a three-minute excerpt, because whoever reads it has to find the relevant part themselves.

**Write down what it contains.** Date, place, who was present, and what is said at which timestamp. A file with that note attached lands differently from a file on its own.

## Don't leave it on one device

A phone gets lost, breaks, or gets wiped, and everything on it goes at once. That matters most for files you won't need until months later.

TalkSafe uses the **Android share function**, so a recording can be sent to any app that accepts a file — emailed to yourself, pushed to cloud storage, moved to a computer.

And this connects back to the first point. **Sending a file to yourself is storage. Sending it to someone else is distribution.** Same button, different act.

## Most recordings are never used

That is the normal outcome, not a failure.

A recording is made in case it is needed. If the case never arrives, the file stays where it is. A folder of recordings and nothing that ever came of them is the best possible result.

Problems tend to start when a recording gets used **without a reason to use it** — sent in anger, posted out of frustration, played to someone to prove a point. At that moment it stops being a defensive record and becomes something else, and the legal analysis shifts with it.

## Where to submit it

It depends on the matter.

**Employment** — a labour agency, a tribunal, or a court. If an internal company process comes first, it is worth checking [what your employer's policy can and cannot do](/blog/en/workplace-recording-policy/) before handing anything over.

**Contract or consumer disputes** — a consumer body, mediation, or civil court.

**Criminal matters** — law enforcement.

In all of them, the three things above — the relevant section, the preserved original, and a written note — make a submission far easier to act on.

## The short version

Four things after a recording: **keep the original**, **trim to the part that matters**, **put one copy off the device**, and **don't circulate it**.

The first three make the file usable. The last one keeps it from coming back at you.
